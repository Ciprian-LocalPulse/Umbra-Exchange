# Manifesto: Umbra Exchange — Toward an Architecture of Trust Without Exposure

### A Philosophical, Technical, and Ethical Foundation for a Zero-Knowledge Protocol for Cyber Threat Intelligence Exchange

**Author: Ciprian Ștefan Pleșca**
*Independent Researcher, Romania*

---

## Abstract

This paper sets out the conceptual foundations, technical architecture, and ethical vision behind **Umbra Exchange** — an open-source protocol for the verifiable yet anonymous exchange of Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) among organizations, security teams, and independent researchers. The paper's central thesis can be stated simply, though its implications are far-reaching: **collective trust should not have to cost individual confidentiality**. The greatest obstacle to effective collaboration between organizations in cybersecurity today is not technical but institutional and psychological — fear of exposure, of legal liability, of eroded competitive advantage, or of public stigma following disclosure of an incident. Umbra Exchange proposes a solution that asks no participant to sacrifice anonymity in order to contribute to the common good: through zero-knowledge proofs, Merkle trees, reputation accumulators, and private set intersection, the system allows an organization to prove that it observed a given threat indicator, at a given level of trust, without revealing its identity, its internal infrastructure, or the true scale of the incident behind the observation. This paper argues that this class of architecture is not merely a point solution to one problem, but a general model for how institutions can cooperate in a digital world governed by structural distrust.

## 1. Introduction: The Paradox of Cooperation in Cybersecurity

For more than two decades, cybersecurity has faced a fundamental paradox that the literature commonly calls *the collective action problem in cyber threat intelligence*. The more organizations share information about the attacks they have suffered, the more resilient the entire ecosystem becomes: an indicator of compromise identified at one bank can prevent an identical attack at another; a malware signature discovered inside a hospital network can protect a critical energy grid. The collective benefit of sharing threat information is, in theory, uncontested.

And yet, in practice, most organizations hesitate to contribute. The reasons are numerous and well documented: fear of publicly revealing that they were compromised, with implications for reputation, market value, and customer trust; legal risk tied to disclosure and reporting obligations (particularly in jurisdictions with strict data-protection regimes, such as the GDPR in the European Union); the potential loss of competitive advantage should a rival learn internal infrastructure details from the context of a reported incident; and, not least, structural distrust of the centralized entities that would aggregate this data — whether governmental, commercial, or non-profit.

The result of this paradox is a fragmented ecosystem in which the most valuable threat intelligence remains locked inside the organizations that hold it, while malicious actors — who suffer none of the same institutional constraints — collaborate freely, buying and selling tools, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) on underground markets with none of the comparable friction. The asymmetry is stark: defenders are fragmented by their own governance structures, while attackers are united by a shared interest in illicit profit.

Existing solutions — platforms such as MISP (Malware Information Sharing Platform), or structured exchange standards such as STIX/TAXII — solve the problem of *how information is formatted* for exchange, but not the problem of *trust* that precedes the decision to contribute in the first place. They implicitly assume participating organizations are already willing to reveal their identity, and often significant details about their own infrastructure, in exchange for access to the aggregated intelligence. Umbra Exchange starts from the opposite premise: **what if the cost of disclosure could be eliminated entirely, while preserving the integrity and verifiability of the information itself?**

## 2. Cryptographic Foundations: From Institutional Trust to Mathematical Trust

The answer Umbra Exchange proposes is not organizational or governance-based — it is not a new alliance, a new consortium, or a new set of compliance rules — but a fundamentally technical one, grounded in a class of cryptographic tools known as **zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs)**. Introduced theoretically by Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff in their seminal 1985 paper, "The Knowledge Complexity of Interactive Proof Systems," zero-knowledge proofs allow one party (the *prover*) to convince another party (the *verifier*) that a statement is true, without revealing any information beyond the truth of the statement itself.

This property, which for decades remained largely a theoretical exercise in mathematical computer science, has over the last ten years become a practical engineering tool, thanks to the emergence of succinct non-interactive zero-knowledge argument systems (zk-SNARKs) — including the **Groth16** protocol, the one actually used in Umbra Exchange's implementation. A zk-SNARK allows the generation of an extremely compact proof (a few hundred bytes, regardless of the complexity of the underlying statement) that can be verified in constant time, with no further interaction between the two parties.

In Umbra Exchange's architecture, this capability is used to build four foundational pillars:

### 2.1. Proof of Observation

A contributor — an organization, a Security Operations Center (SOC), an independent researcher — who has observed an indicator of compromise (a malicious IP address, a file hash, a domain associated with a phishing campaign) can construct a cryptographic proof attesting that this indicator belongs to a data structure known as a *Merkle tree*, previously published for a given time window (an *epoch*). The arithmetic circuit implementing this proof uses the Poseidon cryptographic hash function — chosen specifically for its efficiency inside SNARK circuits, in contrast to functions such as SHA-256, which are considerably more expensive to represent arithmetically. The result is a proof that "this indicator was observed by someone holding a valid secret," without revealing who that someone is.

### 2.2. Preventing Double-Counting via Nullifiers

A central problem in any anonymous contribution system is preventing abuse through duplicate submission: what stops a single contributor from submitting the same observation dozens of times, artificially inflating the confidence score attached to an indicator? Umbra Exchange's solution is the derivation of a *nullifier* — a unique cryptographic value, deterministically derived from the contributor's secret and the observed indicator, published alongside every proof. If the same nullifier appears twice, the second contribution is automatically rejected, without the system ever learning *who* attempted the duplicate — only the *fact* that it had already been recorded.

### 2.3. Anonymous Credential Tiers

Not every contribution carries the same epistemic weight. An observation reported by a certified CERT (Computer Emergency Response Team) reasonably deserves more weight than an anonymous, unvalidated observation. Umbra Exchange resolves this tension — between the need for qualitative differentiation and the principle of anonymity — through a second Merkle tree, published by a trusted issuer, whose leaves contain hashed (secret, trust-tier) pairs. The contributor proves, within the same cryptographic circuit, that their trust tier is at least equal to a claimed threshold, without revealing the exact tier or the identity under which it was certified. This is a meaningful technical achievement: the system can differentiate information quality without compromising source anonymity — a combination that most existing systems treat as mutually exclusive.

### 2.4. Private Set Intersection

Beyond public contribution to a shared ledger, many organizations need to check, on a one-off basis, whether a specific indicator appears on another organization's private threat list — for instance, two competing banks wanting to learn whether they were targeted by the same attacker, without revealing their full lists of compromised indicators to each other. Umbra Exchange implements, for exactly this scenario, a private set intersection protocol based on Diffie-Hellman key exchange over the Ristretto255 group — a deliberate cryptographic choice, rigorously analyzed to avoid an entire class of subtle vulnerabilities associated with naively mapping items to group elements (a mistake that, had it been made, would have completely broken the protocol's confidentiality guarantee, turning a supposed mathematical guarantee into an illusion of security).

## 3. From Circuit to Institution: The Role of the Relay and Interoperability

A cryptographic proof, however mathematically elegant, remains inert without infrastructure to verify it, aggregate it, and make it available to those who need it. Umbra Exchange therefore includes a reference relay service, which performs three essential functions: it verifies the validity of every incoming proof (rejecting any false or malformed submission); it aggregates confidence scores attached to each indicator, weighted according to each contributor's cryptographically-proven trust tier; and it exports the aggregated results in the standard **STIX 2.1** (Structured Threat Information Expression) format, ensuring interoperability with existing security operations tooling without requiring consuming organizations to adopt a new technology stack.

An essential conceptual distinction, treated with particular rigor throughout the system's architecture, is worth emphasizing: **cryptographic proof of belonging to a given trust tier does not, by itself, imply that the trust tier in question originates from a trustworthy source**. The circuit can only prove internal consistency — "this secret corresponds to tier X within this particular tree" — it cannot prove that the tree in question was published by an entity that performed rigorous verification of its contributors' identities. This remains, deliberately and transparently acknowledged in the project's documentation, a matter of institutional governance, not one that mathematics alone can resolve. The relay, accordingly, maintains an explicit allowlist of credential-tree roots it considers trustworthy, and any higher-tier claim originating from a tree not on that list is treated, by design, as base-tier — a design decision that reflects a broader principle underlying this paper: **cryptography establishes the boundaries of the possible; human governance remains responsible for the boundaries of the desirable**.

## 4. Ethical Foundations: Anonymity as a Precondition for Solidarity

Beyond its technical apparatus, Umbra Exchange is, at its core, an ethical proposal. The central argument can be stated as follows: **in the absence of a structural guarantee of anonymity, the demand for transparency paradoxically becomes an obstacle to collective transparency itself**. In other words, when a system requires every organization to individually bear the risk of exposure in order to contribute to the common good, the aggregate result is systematic under-contribution — each organization rationally concludes, individually, that the marginal benefit of contributing does not justify the reputational or legal risk assumed, and the sum of these individually rational decisions produces a collectively irrational outcome: a threat intelligence ecosystem far poorer than it could be.

This observation is not new to game theory or to the economics of public goods — it is, in essence, an applied variant of the *tragedy of the commons* described by Garrett Hardin, and later complicated by Elinor Ostrom's work on the governance of common-pool resources. What Umbra Exchange proposes is a structurally different solution from the two classical approaches — coercive regulation (legally mandating incident reporting) and privatization (creating commercial markets for threat intelligence) — a third way, in which **the technical infrastructure itself eliminates the cost of contribution**, rather than attempting to offset that cost through external incentives or sanctions.

There is, of course, an inherent philosophical tension in any system that combines anonymity with accountability — a tension this paper's author does not minimize, but addresses directly. Complete anonymity, with no accountability structure whatsoever, can itself become a vector for abuse: what assures us that anonymously contributed information is accurate, rather than deliberate disinformation or uncorrected serious error? Umbra Exchange's answer to this legitimate question is the layering of trust — not through identification, but through *cumulative cryptographic reputation*: a contributor can, over time, build a verifiable track record of accurate contributions, attested by their credential tier, without that track record ever being tied to a concrete legal or organizational identity. This is a form of *pseudonymous* accountability, not anonymity in the absolute sense — a subtle but essential distinction for correctly understanding the architecture.

## 5. Epistemic Honesty as Method: Documenting Limitations

A distinctive feature of the Umbra Exchange project, and one the author considers central to its scientific credibility, is the deliberate refusal to overstate the system's degree of maturity. The project's documentation includes a file dedicated exclusively to the threat model (`THREAT_MODEL.md`), which explicitly enumerates not only what the system protects against, but also what it does **not yet** protect against — a rare practice in the public communication of technology projects, where the dominant tendency is to minimize or conceal limitations.

Among the limitations explicitly acknowledged: the absence, as of this writing, of a real, multi-party trusted setup ceremony for the Groth16 cryptographic parameters — any key generated locally, including for testing purposes, remains theoretically vulnerable to a party that retained the "toxic waste" of the setup process; the absence of a finalized governance solution for the credible issuance of higher trust-tier credentials; and known limitations in indicator normalization, which does not yet cover every possible representation of the same underlying data (for instance, "defanged" forms of malicious domains, conventionally used within the security community to prevent accidental link activation).

This practice of epistemic honesty is not a minor detail, but reflects a broader philosophical position held by the author: **in a field where the stakes of a cryptographic error are not ordinary bugs but illusions of security — systems that appear safe and are not — the only intellectually responsible posture is one of radical transparency about one's own limitations**. A cryptographic system that conceals its limitations is, in the author's view, more dangerous than a system that makes no claim to security at all, because the former induces false confidence, which can lead its users to make operationally dangerous decisions based on risks they do not actually understand.

## 6. The Author's Vision: Ciprian Ștefan Pleșca and the Umbra Exchange Project

From its author's perspective, Umbra Exchange is more than a technical tool — it is a statement about how technology can mediate relationships between institutions operating under structural distrust, without requiring them to surrender their autonomy. Ciprian Ștefan Pleșca, an independent researcher from Romania, conceived this project starting from a simple observation, rarely stated explicitly in the cybersecurity literature: **most collaboration failures in cyber defense are not failures of will, but failures of architecture**. Organizations do not refuse to cooperate out of indifference to the common good; they refuse because existing information-sharing infrastructures require them to bear disproportionate individual risk relative to the marginal benefit of their contribution.

The author's vision situates itself within a broader tradition of applied cryptography as a tool of institutional emancipation — a line of thought that can be traced from David Chaum's foundational work on anonymous payment systems, through the development of verifiable electronic voting protocols, to contemporary applications of zero-knowledge proofs in domains such as self-sovereign digital identity and decentralized finance. What distinguishes Pleșca's contribution in this context is the specific application of these tools to a concrete and urgent institutional problem — cooperation in cybersecurity — at a historical moment when cyber threats have significantly outpaced institutions' capacity to cooperate effectively against them.

It is significant, from the author's perspective, that this project is developed entirely in the open, under a permissive license (Apache 2.0), and made available to the entire international cybersecurity community without any commercial restriction. This choice reflects the author's conviction that critical infrastructure for collective trust — much like the internet's own foundational protocols — should not be the exclusive property of a single commercial or national entity, but a digital commons, built through open contribution and subject to constant public scrutiny. This position aligns with the broader tradition of the free and open-source software movement, but applied here with a specific stake: infrastructure for collective trust in the defense against cyber threats is, by its very nature, a domain in which the benefits of commercial centralization are significantly outweighed by the risks of concentrating control over the flow of critical security information.

The author does not claim — and this must be stated with the same epistemic honesty discussed above — that Umbra Exchange represents, at its current stage, a complete solution ready for deployment at scale in real critical infrastructure. As of this manifesto's writing, the project is at an advanced stage of technical development — a functional, rigorously tested cryptographic core, with all foundational primitives implemented and verified — but significant open problems remain in institutional governance, operational scalability, and, most importantly, independent cryptographic audit, without which no security claim can, in the strict scientific sense, be considered validated. The author's vision is not that of a finished product, but of a **conceptual and technical foundation** that the cybersecurity research and practitioner community can take up, examine critically, contest where necessary, and ultimately extend toward an implementation worthy of the real trust of the institutions it aims to serve.

## 7. Broader Implications: Toward a General Theory of Cryptographically Mediated Cooperation

Beyond its specific application to cyber threat intelligence, Umbra Exchange's conceptual architecture suggests a broader class of solutions to a wide category of contemporary institutional problems, which we might generically call **cooperation problems under structural distrust**. The same logical structure — proving a statement without revealing the information that would make the statement exploitable — can be applied, with appropriate adaptation, to domains such as: collaborative regulatory-compliance verification between competing financial institutions, without mutual disclosure of client portfolios; epidemiological coordination between public health systems, without exposing individual medical data; or distributed verification of critical supply-chain integrity, without disclosing confidential commercial relationships between suppliers.

What all of these applications share is an identical epistemic structure: the existence of information that one party holds and would, in principle, be willing to contribute to a collective verification or aggregation system, but is prevented from doing so by the costs associated with directly exposing that information. Zero-knowledge proofs offer, for this entire class of problems, a rigorous mathematical instrument for separating two components otherwise inseparably bound together — the informational utility of a statement, and the risk associated with its full disclosure.

It is the final thesis of this paper that **the development of cryptographic infrastructure for institutional cooperation represents, for the coming decades, a field of importance comparable to the development of foundational networking protocols in decades past** — infrastructure invisible to the end user, yet essential to the coherent functioning of an increasingly institutionally fragmented, yet functionally interdependent, digital society. Umbra Exchange, in its current state as an actively developed open-source project, aims to be a concrete, verifiable, and honestly documented contribution to this broader direction of research and practice.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Umbra Exchange does not, by itself, resolve the crisis of collaboration in global cybersecurity — no purely technical solution can fully resolve a problem whose roots run as deep in institutional, legal, and cultural soil as they do in technical soil. What the project does accomplish, through concrete, rigorously tested implementation, is to demonstrate that **the apparently irreconcilable tension between anonymity and accountability, between confidentiality and verifiability, can be defused through rigorous mathematical tools, rather than through fragile institutional compromise**. This demonstration — that it is cryptographically possible for an organization to contribute to a common good without individually bearing the risk of exposure — represents, regardless of this particular project's specific trajectory, a durable conceptual contribution to how we think about the architecture of collective trust in the digital age.

Ciprian Ștefan Pleșca's vision, expressed through this project, is one of a digital world in which institutions can cooperate not because they trust one another — an assumption often unrealistic in today's competitive and geopolitical context — but because **mathematics itself guarantees the limits of what each party can learn about the others**. This is a form of trust that depends not on institutional goodwill, coercive regulation, or the reputation of any central entity, but on properties that are verifiable, public, and permanently subject to open-community audit. In an era marked by the progressive erosion of classical institutional trust, this proposal — trust as a mathematical property rather than a fragile social relationship — deserves, in this paper's author's view, the serious attention of the international academic and professional cybersecurity community.

---

## Final Note on the Project's Current Status

In the spirit of the epistemic honesty discussed at length in this paper, it bears explicit restatement: as of this manifesto's writing, Umbra Exchange remains a **research prototype, not independently audited**, with a functional cryptographic core tested exhaustively at both unit and integration level, but without a real, multi-party trusted setup ceremony, and without a finalized governance solution for the issuance of trusted credentials. Any use of this system in production contexts involving real, sensitive data should be preceded by an independent cryptographic audit and a deliberate resolution of the institutional governance questions described in this paper. Publishing this manifesto does not constitute a claim that these conditions have already been met — it is an invitation to the research community to help meet them.
